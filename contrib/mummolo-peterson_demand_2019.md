# <Demand Effects in Survey Experiments>

**Contributor:** <Daniel Seungwon Kang>

**Full citation:** <Mummolo, Jonathan, and Erik Peterson. 2019. "Demand Effects in Survey Experiments: An Empirical Assessment." American Political Science Review 113 (2): 517–29.>


## 1. What the paper establishes

The paper deals with the issue of experimenter demand effects (EDEs, a.k.a. the Hawthorne effect) in creating bias in surveys. The research asks whether EDEs are prevalent or not in the context of online surveys, which samples are considered to be equipped with high capacity to produce EDEs. 

This research replicates previously well-known survey experiment designs, and randomly assigns sample respondents to the control and treatment groups, which tests the effect of EDEs when participants are given no information, a directional hint, or an explicit description of the researcher's hypothesis. This paper finds no substantial evidence that EDEs were in effect even when respondents were provided with explicit descriptions or even financial incentives for supporting the hypothesis. 


## 2. Checks for a reviewer

(a) \textbf{Check whether a paper analyzing experimenter-demand effects (EDEs) acknowledges self-administration of online samples within the survey setting or not.} The authors of this paper clearly define the scope of the subjects of the survey to which it may have an effect on the research outcome. The paper does not establish that EDEs are absent from all experiments generally.

(b) \textbf{Check whether the assumptions for differences-in-differences were stated}  Different identification strategies require different assumptions for validity. A cross-sectional comparison requires the assumptions that the control and treatment groups are comparable and have no confounding variables (independent of each other). A differences-in-differences strategy requires that there is no anticipation of the treatment prior to assignment, and that there is a parallel time trend. 

(c) \textbf{Check whether the paper took into account the pure effect of the intended treatment through a preliminary step.} A survey experiment result may be confounded with an existing high saturation of samples that are highly informed with information related to the survey, which may have an effect on the outcome. 

(d) \textbf{If the paper states evidence for effects (or null) with treatments, check whether the paper took into account a situation in which the intended effect would be created at the plausible maximum capacity.} Finding evidence that confirms a hypothesis without setting itself up for a "hard test" does not produce scientific contributions to the literature. 


## 3. Evidence

For (a). The authors first acknowledge that previous literature concerns over the usage of online subject pools who are thought to potentially have high capacities of producing EDEs (p.517). However, the authors find little evidence that validates such concerns of distortions and bias from EDEs in survey experiments (ibid). To test such concerns, the authors acknowledge the prevalent concerns over online samples and define survey experiments as research "in which research subjects self-administer a survey instrument" (p.518).

For (b). The authors define that the key quantity of interest in the experiments is the difference-in-differences. What the authors seek to find is the effect of revealing hints and information about the survey hypothesis (the treatment) (p.522). The treatment is not anticipated prior to the experiment since it is randomly distributed (p.521) and the parallel time trend is assumed along the lines that the presence of the treatment would have altered responses in the samples (p.522).

For (c). The authors checked for the issue of high capacity of online survey respondents to provide researchers "wanted answers." Therefore, to enhance the robustness of the research design, there was a verification of respondents by asking for the "correct" hypothesis of the research purpose (p.522). The mean rate of correctly guessing the hypothesis was 33 percent, and the treatment groups all had a higher rate than the controls. This showed that groups that received a hint or explicit information were informed of the research direction and had the most potential to provide answers that are in line with the hypothesis. However, despite checking that the treatment groups were actually more informed, the survey results did not support the hypothesis that providing information would induce EDEs (p.524). 

For (d). The authors design a follow-up mechanism that sets themselves up for a "hard test" by inducing the treatment group with financial incentives. This mechanism arbitrates between the issues of cognitive limitations and the indifference of samples towards the surveys (p.524). The financial incentive was chosen based on proven sufficiency to change participant behavior (ibid.). The consistent finding of this test would validate the previous test that even the maximization of incentives within relatively simple survey designs are not able to produce EDEs, it can be inferred that it is more likely that EDEs will not be produced in more external complex experimental designs (p.525).


## 4. Scope

\textbf{Applies to:} survey research where respondents are self-administered in an online-setting where respondents are not susceptible to cues from the experimenter. The participants receive experimental treatments and provide outcomes through a survey setting. Checks (a) and (d) applies to such papers described above. Check (b) applies when the paper's main purpose is to reveal the estimated treatment effect across different conditions (p.524).  Check (c) applies only in instances the treatment has been publicized and are known to be common knowledge among the target samples. 

\textbf{Does not apply to:} identification strategies of cross-section comparisons, before-and-after comparisons. The paper explicitly states that the estimand of the design "captures the degree to which demand effects are consequential for the conclusions produced by survey experimental research" (p.522). 

\textbf{Does not apply to:} certain topics (e.g. especially sensitively or normatively charged) or research settings (e.g. face-to-face) that convey demand characteristics (pp.518, 528). 


## 5. What failure looks like

A paper claims to analyze the estimated treated effects but does not report the used identification strategy and needed assumptions for their usage. It does not estimate and report whether the treatment effect was understood or noticed by the treatment group, which leaves out the possibility that high saturation could have altered generalization of a null(or statistically significant) result. It does not report further designs from the first key investigation that would put the experiment against a "hard test." If no hard tests validate the first polled estimates, the inference derived cannot be directly taken as a plausible conclusion and the reader cannot tell whether the first treatment condition produced the effect (or not) on its own.


## 6. Test cases

\textbf{SHOULD trigger:} Kao, Jay C. 2026. "How the Pro-Beijing Media Influences Voters: Evidence from a Randomized Field Experiment." \textit{American Journal of Political Sciences}. 120(3): 795-814. The research conducts a randomized field survey experiment on self-identified samples, in which they are randomly tested to see whether the exposure to pro-Beijing media influences their political attitudes by the indicator of the vote (pp.798-800). The author also derives the treatment effects through the estimands of the intent-to-treat (ITT) and treatment-on-the-treated effects (TOT) (p.804). The paper also sets itself up for hard tests with effect heterogeneity, which provides tests the inferences made with ITT and TOTs (p.807).

\textbf{Should NOT trigger:} Gruffydd-Jones, Jamie J. 2025. "Call Me By Your Name: The Impacts of American Human Rights Violations in Authoritarian States." \textit{British Journal of Political Science} 55(e51): 1-21. The research conducts a survey experiment regarding the effect of a message of U.S. violation on human rights to the individual attitudes of Chinese people towards the CCP and domestic human rights norms. The survey experiment is conducted online with Chinese citizens, but the identification strategy for the treatment effect is not stated, and only provides a figure with the inferred "average treatment effect" (p.13). Moreover, there was no manipulation check that was held prior to the survey, which could have checked for manipulation or saturation effects within the survey. U.S. news would have been widely spread throughout the media that Chinese citizens tend to regularly, and the control group's "generic" information pieces (p. 11) could have been used to infer American violations of human rights, which could effect the conclusion.
